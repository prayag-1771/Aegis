1.1 The real-world problem

Financial crime in India is not one crime. It is three separate crimes that are usually run by the same people:

CrimeWhat actually happens
Scam calls & messagesSomeone phones you pretending to be CBI, police, or your bank. They frighten you into transferring money or sharing an OTP.
Mule account networksThe stolen money doesn't sit in one account. It is instantly split across dozens of accounts owned by ordinary people paid a small fee — "mules" — so it becomes impossible to trace.

Today, each of these is investigated by a different department, using different systems, that never talk to each other. The scam-call complaint sits in one place, the suspicious bank accounts in another, the seized fake notes in a third.

The core insight behind Aegis The criminals are one organisation. The response is three disconnected ones. Aegis connects the three — so a scam call in Jamtara, a mule ring in Alwar, and a fake note seized in Deoghar can be recognised as one operation.

2 · Fraud Rings

This is the most technically ambitious part of Aegis, and where the Research Lab lives.

2.1 The crime: what is a mule network?

When a scammer steals ₹5,00,000 from a victim, they face a problem: a single large transfer is easy to trace and freeze. So they do this instead:

  1. The victim's money goes into account A (the "collector")
  2. Within minutes it is split into 8 smaller transfers to accounts B, C, D…
  3. Each of those splits again, and again
  4. After 3–4 hops the money is withdrawn as cash or sent abroad

Those intermediate accounts belong to real people — often students or unemployed people paid ₹2,000 to hand over their bank card. They are called money mules. A group of them working together is a mule ring.

Why this is hard to catch No single transaction looks suspicious. ₹48,000 moving between two ordinary accounts is completely normal. The crime is only visible in the shape of the whole network — dozens of accounts, all opened recently, all moving money in a tight cycle within minutes.

2.2 What is a "graph"? (the key idea)

In computing, a graph has nothing to do with bar charts. It means a set of things and the connections between them.

  • Node (or vertex) = a thing. Here: a bank account.
  • Edge = a connection. Here: a transaction from one account to another.
Analogy — a social network On Facebook, each person is a node and each friendship is an edge. You can ask questions like "who has the most friends?" or "which people form a tight cluster who all know each other?" Aegis asks exactly those questions about bank accounts and money. A mule ring is simply a tight cluster where the money goes round in a circle.

2.3 Graph vocabulary you need first

Before the detection steps, learn six words. Every one of them appears in the code and on the dashboard, and each one describes something a criminal actually does.

a) Direction — money has a direction

A friendship on Facebook goes both ways. A payment does not. If A pays B, that is not the same as B paying A. So the transaction graph is a directed graph: every edge is an arrow, drawn from the payer to the receiver.

A ──────▶ B A paid B. B did NOT pay A.

In the data, every transaction row has a source (who paid) and a target (who received). That single distinction is what makes it possible to talk about where money enters a network and where it leaves.

b) Degree — how many connections a node has

The degree of a node is simply how many edges touch it. Because our edges are arrows, it splits in two:

TermMeaningWhat a high value suggests
in-degreeHow many different accounts paid into this oneMoney is being gathered here. Typical of a collector.
out-degreeHow many different accounts this one paid out toMoney is being scattered from here. Typical of a distributor.
Analogy — a post office Count the letters arriving at a building (in-degree) and the letters leaving it (out-degree). A normal house has a few of each. A building receiving 200 letters a day and sending 200 straight back out isn't a house — it's a sorting office. In a bank network, that pattern is a mule.

c) Source and sink

These two words come from thinking of money as water flowing through pipes.

TermDefinitionIn a laundering network
SourceA node where money enters the network — much flows out, little or nothing flows inUsually the victim's account, or the first account the stolen money lands in
SinkA node where money leaves — much flows in, little flows back outThe collector or cash-out point: the last account before the money becomes cash or goes abroad
Analogy — a river system A source is the spring where the river begins. A sink is the sea where it ends. Everything in between is just channels moving water along. In a mule network the middle accounts are the channels — and finding the sink is what an investigator actually wants, because that is where the money stopped and where an arrest becomes possible.
Why this matters for the money trail Section 5.1 describes tracing a victim's ₹49,999 into account acc_02033. In graph terms: the victim's account is the source, and Aegis followed the arrow to the sink — the collector inside a detected ring. That is exactly what "the money you lost went here" means.

d) Hub

A hub is a node with an unusually high degree — it touches far more accounts than a normal one. In a laundering ring the hub is nearly always the collector, because dozens of mules all pay into it.

Analogy — an airport Most airports serve a handful of routes. A hub airport connects to hundreds. You can spot a hub without knowing anything about aviation — just by counting connections. Aegis spots collector accounts the same way, via the degree_centrality feature.

e) Throughput — the mule's signature

The single most telling number. Throughput ratio = how much of what came in went straight back out.

  • A normal person receives a salary and spends it gradually. Money accumulates and drains. Ratio well below 1.
  • A mule receives ₹50,000 and forwards ₹49,000 within minutes, keeping a small fee. Ratio close to 1.

Two related features sharpen the picture, and the code comments explain them exactly this way:

FeatureApproaches 1 when…Meaning
fan_in_ratiomany senders, one or few receiversCollector — the sink of the ring
fan_out_ratioone or few senders, many receiversDistributor — the splitter

f) Path, cycle and component

TermMeaning
PathA route following arrows from one account to another: A → B → C. This is how money is traced.
CycleA path that returns to where it started: A → B → C → A. Money going in a circle is almost never innocent — legitimate payments have a purpose and an endpoint.
Connected componentA group of accounts reachable from each other, with no links to the rest. A ring is usually a small, tight component.

2.4 The three shapes a laundering network takes

Aegis can inject any of three topologies live during a demo, and the evaluation reports accuracy per shape — because each one hides money differently.

Shape 1 — Fan-in (the collector)

mule ──┐ mule ──┤ mule ──┼──▶ COLLECTOR ──▶ cash out mule ──┤ (a SINK: high in-degree, low out-degree) mule ──┘

Many accounts pay into one. That one account is the sink. Easiest shape to spot — its in-degree is glaringly abnormal — which is exactly why sophisticated criminals avoid it.

Shape 2 — Chain (layering)

A ──▶ B ──▶ C ──▶ D ──▶ E ──▶ cash out each hop keeps a small fee: 100% → 97% → 94% → 91% …

Money passes hand to hand. No single account looks unusual — each has in-degree 1 and out-degree 1, exactly like a normal person. The crime is only visible in the sequence: the same money moving repeatedly within minutes, shrinking slightly at each step. This is called layering, and it is the hardest shape to detect.

This is why graph analysis is necessary Look at any single account in a chain and it is unremarkable. Traditional rule-based systems examine accounts one at a time — which is precisely why chains defeat them. Only by looking at the shape of the whole network does the chain appear.

Shape 3 — Cycle (the ring)

┌──▶ B ──▶ C ──┐ │ ▼ A ◀── E ◀───── D money returns to where it began

Money moves in a closed loop, sometimes several times, creating a false trail of "business activity" before exiting. The tell is that every account transacts with every other — a high clustering_coefficient, which almost never happens naturally between unrelated people.

How real laundering combines all three

victim ──▶ FAN-OUT ──▶ CHAINS (layering) ──▶ FAN-IN ──▶ cash out SOURCE split it hide the trail gather it SINK

This is the pattern the adversarial simulator in section 2.7 actually generates: fan-out → mule chain → fan-in. The evolving "criminal" tunes how many splits, how many hops and how long the delays are — searching for the combination that escapes detection.

A strong sentence for your presentation "A mule network is money entering at a source, being split, layered through chains so no individual account looks suspicious, then gathered at a sink for cash-out. Rule-based systems examine one account at a time, so the chain is invisible to them. We analyse the shape — which is where the crime actually lives."

2.5 How Aegis finds the rings — step by step

Step 1: Measure each account (feature extraction)

For every account, the system calculates roughly 18 numbers describing its behaviour. These are called features. The important ones:

FeatureWhat it measuresWhy it signals fraud
throughput_ratioHow much of what comes in goes straight back outA mule keeps almost nothing — money passes through. Ratio near 1.0.
burst_ratioHow tightly packed the transactions are in timeReal people transact through the day; laundering happens in minutes.
round_amount_ratioHow often amounts are round numbers₹50,000 exactly, repeatedly, is a script — not a human buying groceries.
degree_centralityHow many other accounts it deals withCollector accounts touch unusually many others.
clustering_coeffWhether its partners also deal with each otherIn a ring, everyone transacts with everyone — a closed loop.

Step 2: Score each account (XGBoost)

XGBoost is the learning algorithm that turns those 18 numbers into one score: the probability this account is illicit.

Analogy — how XGBoost works Imagine a panel of hundreds of simple judges. Judge 1 only knows one rule: "if throughput is above 0.9, add suspicion." Judge 2: "if the account is under 30 days old, add a little more." Each judge alone is nearly useless. But each new judge is specifically trained to fix the mistakes the previous ones made, and their votes are added up. Hundreds of weak rules combine into a very accurate answer. That's "gradient boosting" — the technique behind XGBoost.

Step 3: Group the suspicious accounts into rings (Louvain)

Now we have many suspicious accounts, but which ones belong to the same gang? This uses community detection — specifically the Louvain algorithm.

It looks for groups of nodes that are densely connected to each other but sparsely connected to everyone else. In a school, it would find friendship groups. Here, it finds the ring.

All accounts | | keep only accounts scoring above the risk threshold v High-risk accounts + the transactions between them | | Louvain community detection v Ring 1 (9 accounts) Ring 2 (8 accounts) Ring 3 (11 accounts) ...

2.7 The Research Lab — three experiments

The Research Lab is a tab in the dashboard showing three deeper experiments. Each explores a question the basic system cannot answer. Each is displayed with its honest result, including where it fell short — which is deliberate, and worth pointing out.

Experiment 1 — Ghost Ring (cross-bank detection)

The problem: Criminals deliberately spread accounts across different banks. HDFC sees a quarter of the ring. ICICI sees another quarter. Neither sees a ring — each sees a few odd accounts and dismisses them. And under India's privacy law (the DPDP Act), banks are not allowed to share customer data with each other to compare notes.

The idea: What if banks could collaborate without sharing any customer data?

Analogy — describing a suspect without naming them Four police stations each have one witness. They can't share files. But each can share a coded description — a string of numbers describing behaviour, from which no name or address can be recovered. A central office notices that four descriptions match the same person. Nobody shared a file; everybody learned the truth.

How it works technically:

  1. The transaction network is split into 4 isolated "banks"
  2. Each bank trains its own AI (a GraphSAGE neural network) on only its own data
  3. Each publishes only an embedding — 64 numbers summarising an account's behaviour, from which the original data cannot be reconstructed
  4. A central matcher finds embeddings from different banks that look like two ends of the same transfer
  5. Those matches are stitched together, revealing the complete ring
ResultValueMeaning
Best single bank alone22.9%The most any one bank could see
All four banks fused49.4%2.2× better than any bank alone
False-merge rate0%It never incorrectly joined two unrelated accounts
Differential privacyε = 1.0Strong mathematical privacy guarantee — at zero cost to accuracy
What "differential privacy" means Before an embedding is shared, carefully calibrated random noise is added to it. Enough noise that no individual customer's data can be extracted from it, but not so much that the pattern is lost. ε (epsilon) = 1.0 is a strong guarantee by academic standards. The remarkable finding here is that switching it on did not reduce accuracy at all — usually privacy costs performance.

Experiment 2 — "Criminal Trains the Cop" (adversarial arms race)

The question: Criminals adapt. If our detector becomes known, they will change tactics. Does the detector survive that?

The experiment: A simulated criminal is allowed to evolve. It tries thousands of laundering strategies — more hops, smaller amounts, longer delays — and the ones that evade detection "survive" and are mutated further. This is a genetic algorithm: computational natural selection. Meanwhile the detector periodically retrains on the criminals that escaped.

Analogy — antibiotics and bacteria Bacteria evolve resistance to a drug. Scientists develop a new drug. Bacteria adapt again. This experiment plays out that same war inside a computer, at high speed, to see whether the detector can keep up.

The result: Across 20 generations, the criminal repeatedly erodes the detector's accuracy — and each retraining recovers it. Recall starts at 90.6% and ends at 86.7%, moving up and down in between. That oscillation is the finding: a genuine arms race where neither side wins permanently.

Honest note worth making in the presentation Getting this working required fixing four real bugs, including one where the detector kept forgetting older criminal tactics each time it retrained. Before the fix, recall collapsed to 9%. Saying "our first version failed and here's the bug we found" is far more credible than presenting a clean graph.

Experiment 3 — "Frequency of Fraud" (spectral analysis)

The idea: Borrow a technique from physics and signal processing. Any network has a mathematical "vibration pattern", exactly like a musical instrument has a tone. The claim being tested: a fraud ring vibrates at a different frequency than a normal group of accounts.

Analogy — tapping a bell Tap a solid bell and a cracked bell. Both are bells, but they sound different — the crack changes how vibration travels. Aegis "taps" each community of accounts mathematically and listens. A tight artificial ring rings differently from an organic group of neighbours and shopkeepers.

The measurement is called the Rayleigh quotient — a single number describing how "high-frequency" (irregular) a community's structure is. A fraud ring sits measurably higher than a clean community of comparable size.

The sonification: The dashboard converts these frequencies into actual sound, so you can hear the difference between a clean community and a ring. It is a genuine demonstration of the underlying maths, not decoration.

The stated limitation The panel says openly that comparing communities of different sizes is unreliable — a bigger community naturally scores differently for reasons unrelated to fraud. So the validated claim is narrow: a matched pair (a ring compared to a clean community of similar size) shows a consistent shift. Stating that limit yourself is a strength.

3.2 How a computer reads text — TF-IDF explained

A computer cannot read. It only does arithmetic. So the first job is turning a sentence into numbers.

Part 1: Counting words (TF — term frequency)

Count how often each word appears in the message. "Urgent" appearing twice is a stronger signal than once.

Part 2: Weighting rare words (IDF — inverse document frequency)

Some words appear in nearly every message — "the", "your", "is". They carry no information. Others are rare and therefore meaningful — "arrest", "OTP", "warrant".

Analogy — identifying a person If I tell you a suspect "has two eyes", that's useless — everyone does. If I say they "have a scar on the left eyebrow", that's extremely useful. IDF automatically works out which words are the scars and which are the eyes, by measuring how rare each word is across all messages.

Part 3: Character patterns (char n-grams)

Aegis also examines fragments of letters, not just whole words — every run of 3, 4, and 5 characters. So "urgent", "urgnt" and "u-r-g-e-n-t" all share the fragment "urg", and a scammer's deliberate misspellings don't help them escape.

Why this matters for languages Character fragments are script-agnostic — they work identically on Hindi, Tamil or English letters. This is the technical reason the same model could, in principle, be taught Indian languages directly. Remember this for section 3.7.

Part 4: The decision (Logistic Regression)

Each of the 80,011 word and character patterns has a learned weight — how much it pushes toward "scam" or "safe". To score a message: find which patterns it contains, add up their weights, and convert the total into a probability between 0 and 1.

3.5 Edge AI — the strongest privacy feature

This is the most important thing to understand in this section.

The problem

Normally, scanning a message means uploading it to a server. The citizen's private message — which may contain their name, bank, or account details — leaves their phone. For a government system, that is a serious problem.

The insight

Remember that the "AI" is just a list of 80,011 numbers. It doesn't have to live on a server.

Analogy — the expert's checklist Instead of posting every letter you receive to an expert for review, you get a copy of the expert's checklist once, and check letters yourself at home. The letters never leave your house. That is edge AI: send the model to the phone, not the message to the model.

What was built

The entire model was exported to a file the browser downloads once — 0.9 MB compressed — and the scoring maths was rewritten to run inside the browser.

PropertyResult
AccuracyIdentical — verified to 11 decimal places against the server on 400 messages. Not an approximation: the same numbers, the same arithmetic.
Speed0.8 milliseconds, versus a network round-trip of hundreds of milliseconds — or 50–90 seconds if the free server is asleep.
PrivacyThe message never leaves the browser. Only an anonymised verdict is sent onward.
CostOne 0.9 MB download, then cached.
The demo moment — rehearse this Load the page, turn on aeroplane mode, paste a scam message. It still detects it.

That single action ends the privacy conversation. It isn't a claim the audience has to trust — they watch it work with no network. It also makes your demo immune to bad venue wifi.

3.6 Live Call Shield

Scam calls are more dangerous than messages, because the victim is under live psychological pressure. Live Call Shield scores the conversation as it happens.

3.7 Multilingual support — and a subtle bug worth telling

India has 22 official languages. A scam-detection system that only works in English protects the people who least need protecting.

How it works

The model is trained in English. So for other languages, the message is translated to English first (using Sarvam AI, an Indian company), then scored. The app checks the script: Latin letters are scored on-device; Devanagari, Tamil, Bengali and others go to the server for translation.

11 languages are supported on live calls: English, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, Punjabi and Urdu.

Two real bugs that were found by testing — good material for a presentation

Bug 1 — the translator quietly reversed the meaning The translation service was being asked to auto-detect the language. It refused Urdu entirely, and for Marathi and Telugu it translated "your KYC has expired" into "your KYC is already completed, thank you" — which is harmless English, and was scored as harmless. Urdu and Marathi scams were scoring 0.05 (safe).

Fix: detect the script and tell the translator explicitly which language it is. All six tested languages then scored above 0.8 (scam).
Bug 2 — "Hinglish" was completely invisible Most real Indian scam messages are Hindi written in English letters: "Aapke account mein suspicious transaction detect hua hai… OTP bata dijiye." There is no Hindi character anywhere, so nothing triggered translation — and the English model has never seen "aapka" or "zaroori". It scored 0.076 (safe).

Fix: detect distinctive romanised Hindi words (requiring at least three, and excluding words that are also English like "main" and "hai"), then translate. It now scores 0.64 (scam), and does not false-alarm on ordinary English.
Why this belongs in your presentation This is the single best demonstration that you tested your system rather than assuming it worked. Most teams would demo an English scam and stop. "We tested six Indian languages and found two silent failures — one where the translator inverted the meaning" shows real engineering rigour.

3.8 The agentic verifier — AI that checks, not guesses

Everything so far judges the message by how it is written. This layer does something different: it acts on what the message contains.

When a message is flagged, an AI agent extracts the concrete things a scammer relies on and goes and checks them in the real world:

3.9 WhatsApp channel

Most Indian scam messages arrive on WhatsApp, and the safest thing a citizen can do is forward a suspicious message to a number that checks it — no app to install, no account to make.

Aegis includes a WhatsApp bot: forward a message and it replies with a verdict, the markers it found, and safety advice ("do not click any link, do not share OTP, report on 1930"). There is a simulator page for demos, plus a real Twilio webhook.

A security detail done properly The webhook verifies Twilio's cryptographic signature before trusting a request, so nobody can post fake messages to it by guessing the address. It correctly handles tunnel-forwarding headers, which is a common source of bugs when testing locally.
State it accurately This is a transport adapter with a working simulator, not a bot running on a live business number. The honest framing: "the detection pipeline is complete and signature-verified; connecting a production WhatsApp Business number is a deployment step."

4 · Counterfeit Vision

Photograph a banknote; the system decides whether it is genuine — and shows you why.

4.1 The problem

Fake Indian currency (called FICN — Fake Indian Currency Notes) funds organised crime and terrorism. Detection today relies on trained eyes and machines available only in banks. A shopkeeper or constable in a small town has neither.

4.2 What is a neural network? (very basic)

The earlier modules worked on numbers and words. Images are different: a photo is millions of coloured dots, and no simple rule can turn dots into "this note is fake".

Analogy — how a network learns to see A neural network processes an image in layers. The first layer detects only tiny edges — light meeting dark. The next combines edges into corners and curves. The next combines those into shapes: an eye, a letter, a pattern. The final layer combines shapes into a judgement: "this is Gandhi's portrait" or "this security thread is missing."

Nobody programmed those layers. They were learned from thousands of labelled examples.

4.3 Transfer learning — why this is possible at all

Training a network to see from scratch needs millions of images and enormous computing power. Aegis has a few thousand banknote photos. The solution is transfer learning.

Analogy — hiring an experienced photographer You need someone to spot fake notes. You could raise a child from birth teaching them to see (impossible for you), or hire an experienced photographer who already understands edges, textures and light, and simply teach them the specific patterns of Indian banknotes. That's transfer learning: start from a network already trained on millions of general images, and retrain only its final decision layer.

The network used is EfficientNet-B0 — a well-regarded architecture chosen because it is accurate but small enough to run on modest hardware.

Almost every real note was being called fake.

The fix: the pre-screen became advisory only. It still records what it measured, but only the neural network — trained on real photographs — can decide.

4.6 The heatmap (Grad-CAM) — explainability

A verdict alone is not enough for an officer. They need to know why. The system produces a heatmap overlaid on the note, colouring the regions that most influenced the decision.

Analogy — a teacher marking an essay A grade of 6/10 with no marks on the page is useless. A teacher who underlines the specific sentences that lost marks is teaching. Grad-CAM underlines the parts of the note that drove the verdict.
An engineering compromise worth knowing The standard heatmap technique needs a "backward pass", which roughly triples memory use and crashed the free-tier server mid-scan. So on low-memory machines the system switches to Eigen-CAM, which produces a heatmap using only the forward pass. The verdict is byte-identical either way — only how the picture is computed changes.

4.7 The serial-number registry

A clever extra check. Every genuine note has a unique serial number. Counterfeiters typically print one real serial onto thousands of notes. So Aegis remembers every serial it has seen, and if the same serial appears in two different districts, that is strong evidence of a printing run — a link the image alone could never provide.

4.8 The vision second look — the only layer that reads the note

This one exists because of a real hole that was found, not a theoretical one.

Every other check in Counterfeit Vision measures print physics: ink saturation, intaglio texture, paper sharpness. Even the CNN — it was trained on genuine notes versus physically counterfeited ones, so what it learned to separate is print quality. Nothing in the pipeline reads what is actually printed on the note.

The failure this exposed Take a genuine note and paste a different face over Gandhi. The paper is genuine, the intaglio texture is genuine, the colour is genuine — every physical measurement passes. And the CNN, which shrinks the image to 224×224, pools that one altered region into almost nothing. The system returned "genuine" at 95% confidence on an obviously tampered note.

The fix is a vision LLM — an AI that can look at an image and answer questions about its content:

  • Is the portrait actually Mahatma Gandhi?
  • Does the header read "RESERVE BANK OF INDIA"?
  • Is "SPECIMEN" overprinted anywhere?
  • Does the printed denomination match?
Why this layer is allowed to convict — unlike the others Elsewhere the rule is that only the CNN decides. This layer is the exception, and the reasoning is precise: a wrong portrait or wrong header is a fact about what is printed, not a fragile measurement of a surface. Dim lighting can fool a texture check; it cannot turn someone else's face into Gandhi. So content findings convict, while "SPECIMEN" detected merely caps the verdict to "needs manual check".

5 · The Dashboard and Generative AI

This is where the three modules stop being separate tools and become one intelligence picture.

5.1 Fusion — finding connections

The correlator is a deterministic program (no AI) that looks for real, checkable links between events from different modules.

The strongest link: the money trail

This is the single best result in Aegis, and the one to demonstrate:

  1. A victim reports a scam call, and reports that they paid ₹49,999
  2. The fraud graph independently knows every transaction between mule accounts
  3. The correlator looks for an incoming payment of that same amount (within 1%), arriving shortly after the call
  4. It finds ₹49,999 landing in account acc_02033, part of detected ring ring_05
Why this is powerful Two completely independent systems — one reading text, one reading bank transactions — independently point at the same crime. The dashboard shows this as an orange "money trail traced" card: the money you lost went here. That is what no single department can currently produce.

Other links the correlator finds

  • Scam ↔ ring: a scam and a mule ring in the same district within a time window
  • Scam ↔ counterfeit: both crime types clustering in one place
  • Plate families: fake notes from different districts sharing the same printing defects — the same physical printing plate
  • Scam campaigns: different victims receiving near-identical scripts, or the same callback number — one operation, not isolated incidents

5.2 Where Generative AI is used — and where it is deliberately not

5.3 The fallback chain — why the demo cannot die

AI services fail: they go down, hit rate limits, or get blocked. So every AI feature tries providers in order, and ends with a guaranteed non-AI option:

Claude -> Groq -> Gemini -> Groq (spare) -> TEMPLATE | a plain-English summary written by ordinary code — always works, needs no internet, no key, and cannot fail

The template at the end is the key design decision. Even with zero API keys and no internet, every panel still produces a sensible summary of the same facts. The demo cannot go blank.

Real incidents from this project Two genuine failures were found and fixed: Google retired the Gemini model being used (every call returned "no longer available"), and the timeouts were shorter than the AI needed to write its answer — so every provider was cut off mid-sentence and silently fell through to the template. Both are good examples of why the fallback exists.

5.4 The AI Case Officer

One click on a district produces a complete case brief: a summary, a chronological timeline, a hedged hypothesis, and recommended next steps — each citing the specific evidence it came from.

It works in two strictly separated stages:

  1. Gather evidence (deterministic): collect every scam, ring, seizure and correlation for that district into a dossier of established facts
  2. Write the brief (AI): the LLM reads only that dossier and writes it up. It is forbidden from referring to anything outside it.
Note the careful language The brief always hedges — "consistent with", "suggests", "worth checking" — and never names individuals or asserts guilt. This matters: a system that produces confident accusations about citizens is dangerous. A system that produces investigative leads is useful.

When counterfeit notes are seized, where did they enter the country? Supply Trail uses Dijkstra's algorithm — the same shortest-path method used by map apps — over India's real rail, road and air corridors, to compute plausible routes from a likely origin to the seizure location.

Stated honestly in the product A banknote carries no origin label. The route is a hypothesis for investigators, not evidence. The confidence score is capped below 1.0 by design, and the interface says so explicitly.

6.6 Intel Feed — growing the evidence base from news

Supply Trail is only as good as the seizure records it knows about. Those exist in public reporting — news articles about FICN seizures — but a program cannot be trusted to decide what counts as evidence.

So the pipeline is split deliberately:

  • Offline batch job: fetches articles and extracts candidate facts (district, denomination, amount, date)
  • Intel Feed page: an officer reviews each candidate and chooses which enter the corpus
Two careful decisions It never fetches news during a request — candidates are pre-staged, so nothing waits on an external site and the fusion result stays reproducible.
Every ingest writes a backup, with undo and full reset — so the feature can be driven repeatedly in rehearsal without damaging anything.

6.7 The self-improving classifier

A scam detector trained on yesterday's scams will miss tomorrow's. This experiment asks: can the system prepare for scams that don't exist yet?

The method turns an LLM into the adversary:

  1. An LLM writes brand-new scam variants designed to evade filters trained on classic patterns — paraphrased authority claims, none of the usual keywords, new pressure tactics
  2. Half are added to the training data
  3. The other half are held back as an "unseen future scams" test set
  4. The model is retrained, and measured on that held-out half
Why the split matters If you trained on all the generated scams and then tested on them, you would learn nothing — the model has seen the answers. Holding half back means the improvement is measured against variants the model has genuinely never seen. That is the difference between a demo and an experiment.
Analogy — a vaccine A vaccine shows the immune system a harmless version of a threat so it can recognise the real one later. Here the LLM manufactures harmless scam variants so the classifier learns the shape of an attack it has not encountered.

7 · What Is Genuinely New

Judges ask "what did you invent, versus what did you assemble?" This chapter separates the two honestly. Everything below either does not exist in a shipped product, or exists only as a research paper.

7.1 Cross-domain fusion with a deterministic evidence engine

No product on the market correlates scam detection, counterfeit currency, and fraud-ring intelligence together. Single-domain products exist in each silo — the convergence layer does not.

The architectural novelty is how generative AI is used: the correlation itself is deterministic and reproducible (exact arithmetic on amounts and timestamps), and the LLM only narrates the result. Most "AI fusion" claims invert this — letting the model decide what is linked, which is unauditable.

Described in section 3.8. What is new is that the AI checks the scammer's own claims against the real world — resolving the link, validating the IFSC, checking the UPI provider — instead of only judging the writing style.

Counterfeiters print one real serial number onto thousands of notes. Recording every serial seen means the second sighting of the same serial in a different district is evidence of a printing run — intelligence no image model can produce, and not present in citizen-facing tools.

7.8 The cap-only safety invariant — a design contribution

The rule, in one line A secondary AI layer may only make a verdict less confident, never more.

The serial check and the vision review can downgrade "genuine" to "needs manual check". They can never upgrade "genuine" to "fake", and never acquit a "fake". This means a hallucinating or malfunctioning AI layer can only ever cause extra human review — the safe direction — and never a false accusation.

This is a reusable design principle for adding AI to any high-stakes decision system, and it is worth naming as a contribution in its own right.

7.9 Mid-call intercept — acting before the money moves

Existing systems are post-complaint. Live Call Shield interrupts during the call, before the transfer — the difference between recording a crime and preventing one.

7.10 The money trail — a victim's report to a freezable account

Joining a citizen's reported payment to a specific account inside a detected ring (section 5.1). This is the output an investigator can actually act on: an account number to freeze.

7.11 Coordinated-hub detection on the map

Districts where all three crime types converge are marked as coordinated hubs — a stronger signal than any single-domain hotspot, and only computable because the three feeds share one map.

7.12 Contract-first architecture

An engineering decision that became a product feature: because every module speaks a strict validated schema, modules can be developed independently, and malformed data is rejected at the door rather than corrupting the intelligence picture three steps later.

7.13 Honest-negative reporting

The Research Lab displays results that did not work — the arms race showing detector decay, the spectral method's stated unreliability for cross-community ranking, the federated recall gain labelled unproven.

Why this is listed as an innovation Publishing your own negative results is rare in a hackathon and rare in industry. It is what separates an experiment from a demo. If a judge probes any weakness, they find you already documented it — which converts an attack into evidence of rigour.

12.2 The questions you will be asked

"Does the citizen's message go to your server?" "No. The classifier runs in the browser — the same model, verified score-for-score against the server version. Watch: aeroplane mode, still works."
"What do you send to your servers, then?" "Verdict, scam type, district, timestamp. Not the message. That's everything the national crime map needs, and nothing more."
"Doesn't your AI see the message?" "No. The AI only receives tool results and a redacted excerpt — digits, links and IDs masked. Phone numbers are irreversible hashes, never raw."
"How do you prevent AI hallucination?" "The AI never decides anything. Every verdict comes from a deterministic model or algorithm. The AI only writes the English explanation of facts it is handed. Remove it entirely and every verdict is unchanged."
"Is this just trained on your own made-up data?" "The fraud graph was also benchmarked on Elliptic++ — 265,354 real Bitcoin wallets from a published academic dataset — at 0.9945 AUC. The counterfeit model is trained on real photographs, and the scam model includes a public SMS spam corpus."
"What about languages other than English?" "Eleven languages on live calls. English is scored on the device; other languages are translated and scored on our server. We also detect Hinglish — Hindi written in English letters — which is how most real Indian scam messages are actually written."
"What doesn't work? / What are the limitations?" Answer this one honestly and specifically — it is often the question that decides the result.

"Three things. Investment scams: we catch about half — they read like legitimate financial advice. Live call speech-to-text goes through the browser, so audio reaches Google — production would use on-device speech or telecom integration. And the fraud-ring numbers on synthetic data are flattering, which is why we also benchmarked on real Bitcoin data."
"Could this actually be deployed?" "For SMS, yes — a mobile app can read messages on Android, and iOS has an on-device SMS filter API designed for exactly this. For calls, no app on iOS or Android is permitted to tap call audio, so production would require telecom-operator integration. That's a policy constraint, not a technical one."
